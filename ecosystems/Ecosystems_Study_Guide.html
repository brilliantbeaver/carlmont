🌍Topic 1 · Earth Systems Science

Know it cold: Earth system science is the study of the relationships between air, land, life, and water on our planet. You must be able to (1) put any item into the right sphere and (2) describe how an event in one sphere causes changes in another.

🔤 Key terms in this topic (7) — definitions at a glance
Earth system science
The study of the relationships between air, land, life, and water on Earth — the four connected spheres.
Atmosphere
All the air and gases around Earth, including weather, wind, clouds, climate, and the jet stream.
Hydrosphere
All of Earth's water in every form — oceans, rivers, glaciers, sea ice, snow, soil moisture, aquifers, and water vapor.
Biosphere
All living things — plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, algae, coral reefs.
Geosphere
The solid Earth — rock, soil minerals, volcanoes, plate tectonics, earthquakes, fossils, magnetism.
Closed system
Energy enters and leaves Earth, but very little matter does — so matter must be recycled.
Sphere interaction
An event in one sphere that causes change in another (lightning in the atmosphere ignites the biosphere).

What is Earth system science?

Earth system scientists treat Earth as four connected systems ("spheres"). A change in one sphere ripples into the others. That is why a lightning storm (air) can burn a forest (life), wash soil into creeks (land → water), and change the climate (air) — all from one event.

Big idea What it means Why it matters
Closed system Earth receives energy from the Sun (and loses heat to space), but very little matter enters or leaves. Matter must be recycled — water cycle, carbon cycle, and nutrients returned by decomposers.
The Sun The energy driver of most Earth-system interactions. Powers weather, the water cycle, and photosynthesis (the start of every food chain).
Earth's core Internal heat; the energy driver of many geosphere processes. Powers plate tectonics, volcanoes, earthquakes, and the rock cycle.
The Earth Third planet from the Sun with unique properties that allow life. Liquid water + a protective atmosphere make the biosphere possible.

The four spheres

Sphere Word clue What belongs here (from your class organizer) Watch out!
Atmosphere atmo = vapor/air air, winds, weather, climate, clouds, jet stream, solar-radiation buffer, CO₂/O₂ mixing, heat mixing Anything that is a gas or weather goes here.
Hydrosphere hydro = water ALL water in every form — liquid, solid, gas: oceans, lakes & rivers, the water cycle, sea ice, glaciers, snow cover, soil moisture, aquifers (groundwater) Frozen water and underground water are still hydrosphere. Glaciers are not geosphere.
Biosphere bio = life plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, algae, coral reefs, producers/consumers/decomposers, evolution & adaptation Anything alive — including tiny bacteria and the process of adaptation.
Geosphere geo = earth land, rock, soil minerals, plate tectonics, volcanoes, the rock cycle, hot spots, sedimentation, fossilization, earthquakes, magnetism Fossils, sediment, and Earth's magnetic field are geosphere (per your organizer).

Common trap — soil: the minerals in soil are geosphere, the water in soil (soil moisture) is hydrosphere, and the worms and bacteria in soil are biosphere. Read the question to see which part it is asking about.

How the spheres interact

A strong answer names the cause sphere → the effect sphere: "Lightning in the atmosphere ignited dry plants in the biosphere."

Event Sphere interactions
Photosynthesis Plants (bio) take in CO₂ (atmo) + water (hydro) + minerals (geo) → release O₂ (atmo)
Volcanic eruption Magma (geo) → ash & gases into the air (atmo) → acid rain (hydro) → harms fish and plants (bio)
Hurricane Warm ocean water (hydro) fuels the storm (atmo) → erodes coastline (geo) → destroys habitat (bio)
Lichen on bare rock Lichens (bio) break rock into tiny pieces → first soil (geo) — the start of primary succession
Glaciers carving Yosemite Ice (hydro) scrapes and carves granite (geo) → bare rock where life (bio) must start over
Rain after a fire Rain (hydro) washes burned, bare soil (geo) into creeks (hydro) → muddy water harms fish (bio)

From your handouts — CZU fire as an Earth-systems event: dry lightning (atmosphere) ignited vegetation (biosphere) that had dried out during the hot, dry summer of Big Basin's Mediterranean climate; wind (atmosphere) and steep slopes (geosphere) spread the fire; smoke and CO₂ went into the atmosphere; after the fire, bare soil (geosphere) could wash into streams (hydrosphere).

🤝Topic 2 · Ecological Relationships

Know it cold: Every relationship can be scored from the point of view of both species: + (helped), 0 (not affected), − (harmed). Score both species and the name follows.

🔤 Key terms in this topic (11) — definitions at a glance
Symbiosis
A close, long-term relationship between two different species (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism).
Mutualism (+/+)
Both species benefit — bees & flowers; lichen (fungus + alga).
Commensalism (+/0)
One species benefits; the other is neither helped nor harmed — barnacles on a whale.
Parasitism (+/−)
The parasite benefits by living on or in a host and harming it, usually without killing it quickly — tick on a deer.
Predation (+/−)
A predator hunts, kills, and eats its prey — puma & mule deer.
Predator–prey cycle
Repeating rise and fall of predator and prey numbers; predator peaks come after prey peaks.
Competition (−/−)
Organisms need the same limited resource (food, water, space, light, mates).
Intraspecific competition
Competition within the SAME species — gulls competing for nesting space on a rock.
Interspecific competition
Competition between DIFFERENT species — a lion and a tiger competing for a water buffalo.
Keystone species
A species with a much bigger effect than its numbers suggest; removing it destroys the ecosystem's balance (wolves in Atlas NP; bees).
Trophic cascade
A change at the top of a food web that ripples down through several levels (wolves ↓ → deer ↑ → grass ↓).

Symbiosis: mutualism, commensalism, parasitism

Symbiosis = a close, long-term relationship between two different species. There are three types.

Relationship Score Definition Classic examples Clue words in a question
Mutualism + / + Both species benefit bees & flowers (food ↔ pollination); lichen (fungus gives shelter & water, alga makes food); clownfish & sea anemone; coral & algae "both," "trade," "in exchange," "each gets"
Commensalism + / 0 One benefits; the other is neither helped nor harmed barnacles on a whale; remora riding a shark; bird nesting in a tree; cattle egret eating insects stirred up by cattle "not affected," "neither helped nor harmed," "doesn't notice"
Parasitism + / − One benefits; the other (the host) is harmed tick on a mule deer; flea on a dog; tapeworm in an intestine; mistletoe on a tree "host," "feeds on," "weakens," "lives on/in"

Common traps

  • Parasite vs. predator — both are + / −. A predator kills and eats its prey (usually quickly). A parasite lives on or in its host and feeds on it over time, usually without killing it right away.
  • Commensalism vs. mutualism — ask: "Does the second species get anything?" If yes → mutualism. If nothing changes for it → commensalism.

Predator and prey

Predation (+ / −): the predator hunts, kills, and eats the prey — puma & mule deer, bobcat & brush rabbit, lynx & snowshoe hare, wolves & deer.

Why it matters: predators keep prey populations from growing past what their food supply can handle (predation is a density-dependent limiting factor). Prey, in turn, limit predators: no prey, no food.

Predator–prey cycles — the populations rise and fall in a repeating pattern:

  1. Prey increase → more food for predators.
  2. Predators increase (a little later — there is a lag).
  3. More predators eat more prey → prey decrease.
  4. Less food → predators decrease (again, later).
  5. Fewer predators → prey recover → the cycle repeats (snowshoe hare and lynx: about every 10 years).

From your handouts: rabbits introduced to Australia in the mid-1800s had no predators, so they exploded, ate the land bare, and crashed. The worksheet notes that a predator (the lynx) helped stabilize the rabbit population — predators can keep prey near carrying capacity.

Competition: intraspecific vs. interspecific

Competition (− / −): two organisms need the same limited resource — food, water, space, sunlight, mates, or nesting sites. Both lose something.

Type Word clue Definition Examples
Intraspecific intra = within (like intramural sports inside one school) Competition between members of the same species Seagulls competing for nesting space on a rock (class example); two mule deer bucks competing for mates; redwood seedlings competing for light
Interspecific inter = between (like interstate highways between states) Competition between different species A lion and a tiger competing for a water buffalo (class example); a coyote and a bobcat competing for brush rabbits

Why it matters: competition gets stronger as a population gets more crowded, so it is a density-dependent limiting factor. Intraspecific competition is often the most intense, because members of the same species need exactly the same things.

Keystone species

Keystone species = a species that has a much bigger effect on its ecosystem than its numbers suggest. If it is removed, the ecosystem's balance (equilibrium) is destroyed and the ecosystem can collapse. There can be more than one keystone species in an ecosystem.

A keystone species is NOT necessarily the most abundant species, the biggest species, or always the top predator. Bees (pollinators) and beavers (dam builders) can be keystone species too.

Keystone species What it does What happens without it
Bees (class example) Pollinate plants Plants decline → primary consumers lose food → the decline cascades upward
Wolves (Yellowstone; Atlas NP Gizmo) Keep deer & coyote numbers in check Deer overpopulate and overgraze → grass, bees, ravens decline
Sea otters Eat sea urchins Urchins explode and eat the kelp forest
Pisaster sea stars Eat mussels in tide pools Mussels crowd out most other tide-pool species

Trophic cascade: a change at the top of a food web "cascades" down through several levels (wolves ↓ → deer ↑ → grass ↓ → bees ↓).

From your Gizmo case study (Atlas National Park):

Species Normal range Now 5 yrs after adding bees 5 yrs after adding wolves
Grass 800–1,000 blades/m² 400 350 850
Coyotes 250–300 600 650 270
Ravens 220–270 160 145 245
Deer 4,000–8,000 15,000 17,000 7,000
Bees 4,000–5,000 hives 2,000 3,000 4,250

People might guess bees are the keystone (pollinators are important!), but adding bees made things worse. Adding wolves brought every population back into its normal range → wolves are the keystone species in Atlas National Park.

🍃Topic 3 · Food Chains, Webs & Cycling of Energy

Know it cold: Energy enters as sunlight, producers turn it into chemical energy, and only about 10% passes up each trophic level. The other ~90% is used for metabolism and leaves as heat. Arrows point from the food to the eater.

Metabolism
All the chemical reactions that keep an organism alive; it uses energy and releases some as heat.

A model showing the energy at each trophic level: 100% → 10% → 1% → 0.1%.

Trophic levels: who eats whom

A trophic level ("food level") is an organism's position in a food chain.

Level Name Also called Gets its energy from Big Basin example
1 Producer Autotroph (auto = self, troph = food) Sunlight, by photosynthesis (light energy → chemical energy in sugar) redwood sorrel, California poppy, coast redwood
2 Primary consumer Herbivore (a heterotroph) Producers mule deer, California brush rabbit
3 Secondary consumer Carnivore or omnivore Primary consumers gray fox, bobcat, coyote
4 Tertiary consumer Often the apex (top) predator Secondary consumers (may also eat primary consumers) puma (mountain lion)
5 Quaternary consumer Apex predator Tertiary consumers (rare — few chains are this long)
  • Heterotroph (hetero = other) = any organism that gets food by eating other organisms — every consumer and decomposer.
  • Herbivore eats only producers (deer). Carnivore eats only animals (puma). Omnivore eats both (black bear) — so an omnivore can sit at more than one trophic level.

Tip — trophic level depends on the meal: a puma eating a mule deer is a secondary consumer (level 3). The same puma eating a gray fox is a tertiary consumer (level 4). A black bear eating redwood sorrel is a primary consumer; eating a deer, it is a secondary consumer.

Food chains vs. food webs: reading the arrows

The arrow means "is eaten by" or "gives energy to." It points from the food → to the eater — the direction energy flows.

Predicting ripple effects with a food web — if the California brush rabbit disappeared:

  • Gray fox and bobcat lose their only food in this web → they decline.
  • Coyotes depend more on mule deer → more pressure on deer.
  • Pumas lose fox and bobcat prey and rely on deer.
  • Redwood sorrel and poppy may increase (fewer rabbits eating them).

From your handout — comparing models (Part 4: Model Evaluation):

Model Useful for… Lacks / could cause a misconception…
Food chain One simple, step-by-step path of energy; who eats whom Doesn't show the whole picture — real organisms eat (and are eaten by) many species
Energy pyramid How much energy is at each level; why there are fewer organisms at the top Doesn't show how the organisms are related to each other
Food web The whole ecosystem and how species interact; predicting ripple effects Hard to read with many arrows; doesn't show energy amounts

For a real ecosystem like Big Basin, the food web is the most complete model.

From your handout — disturbance (fire) effects on trophic levels (Part 5): producers + (fire clears debris and small trees → less competition, more sunlight reaches the forest floor); primary consumers neutral (food regrows quickly, but fire and smoke are direct dangers); secondary and tertiary consumers − (habitat loss, fewer prey, poor air quality). Part 5 also defines the abiotic parts of the cards (drought, fire) as ecological forces or disturbances.

Decomposers, scavengers & detritivores

Role What it does Examples
Scavenger Animal that eats dead animals vultures eating a dead raccoon
Detritivore Survives on dead or decaying organisms and wastes earthworms, millipedes, banana slugs
Decomposer Uses enzymes to break down dead organisms and wastes into simple nutrients fungi and bacteria

Why decomposers matter: decomposition returns nutrients to the soil, where they are taken up again by producers. Without decomposers, dead material would pile up and producers would run out of nutrients — the whole food web would starve.

Common traps

  • Decomposers are not primary consumers. They feed on dead material from every trophic level.
  • Energy flows; matter cycles. Nutrients (matter) are recycled back to producers again and again. Energy enters as sunlight and eventually leaves as heat — it cannot be reused. (Your Gizmo handbook titles this section "Cycling of Energy"; on a test, remember that the nutrients are what get recycled.)

Energy flow, metabolism & the 10% rule

Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed on to the next level. Where does the other ~90% go?

  • Metabolism = all the chemical reactions that keep an organism alive (moving, growing, reproducing, staying warm). During metabolism, some chemical energy is converted to heat energy. The heat leaves the organism, goes into the environment, and leaves the food chain.
  • Some energy is never passed on (parts not eaten — bones, fur, wood — and undigested waste go to decomposers).
How to calculate Rule Example
Up one level multiply by 0.10 (÷ 10) 10,000 kcal producers → 1,000 kcal primary consumers (Gizmo)
Up several levels ÷ 10 for each step 3,500,000 → 350,000 → 35,000 → 3,500 kcal (Creating Chains, Q8)
Down (backward) × 10 for each step Tertiary has 50 kcal → secondary 500 → primary 5,000 → producers 50,000 kcal
As a percent of producers 100% → 10% → 1% → 0.1% Tertiary consumers get 0.1% of the producers' energy

Units: energy is measured in calories; 1,000 calories = 1 kilocalorie (kcal).

Why the 10% rule matters:

  1. Fewer organisms at higher levels — lots of plants, fewer rabbits, very few pumas (Q9 on your worksheet).
  2. Food chains are short — usually only 4–5 levels, because energy runs out.
  3. Top predators need huge territories to find enough food, so they are hit hardest by habitat fragmentation.

From your handout: "Three hundred trout are needed to support one man for a year. The trout, in turn, must consume 90,000 frogs, that must consume 27 million grasshoppers that live off of 1,000 tons of grass." — G. Tyler Miller, Jr. (1971). Every step up loses most of the energy.

📈Topic 4 · Limiting Factors & Carrying Capacity

Know it cold: A population grows exponentially when resources are plentiful, overshoots its carrying capacity (K), declines, recovers, and then rises and falls around K in a cyclical/periodic pattern. Limiting factors set K.

🔤 Key terms in this topic (10) — definitions at a glance
Limiting factor
An environmental factor that slows or stops a population's growth — food, space, weather.
Density-dependent factor
Has a stronger effect when the population is crowded — disease, competition, predation, waste.
Density-independent factor
Affects a population the same way at any density — floods, cold fronts, hurricanes, fire. It can still kill many!
Carrying capacity (K)
The largest population an area can support over time with its resources (food, water, space).
Exponential growth
J-shaped growth that gets faster and faster when resources seem unlimited.
Logistic growth
S-shaped growth that slows and levels off at the carrying capacity.
Overshoot
When a population grows above its carrying capacity.
Decline (die-off)
The sharp drop after an overshoot, as resources run out.
Recovery
The population rises again after a decline and stabilizes around K.
Cyclical / periodic pattern
A repeating rise-and-fall pattern — a population fluctuating around K, or predator–prey cycles.

Limiting factors

A limiting factor is an environmental factor that slows or stops a population's growth (or causes it to decrease). Limiting factors can be biotic (food, predators, disease, competitors), abiotic (water, space, sunlight, temperature, weather), or both.

Limiting factor Example from your worksheet The lesson
Food & water A bear must eat 10 fish a day; the river provides about 100 fish a day → the river supports at most 10 bears. Extra shelter or water won't help — food is the limiting factor. 100 ÷ 10 = 10 bears.
Living space Gulls need nesting space on rocky shores; a pair with no room can't raise chicks. Crowded plants run out of root space, and taller plants shade smaller ones. More nesting space → the gull population could increase.
Waste Animal waste pollutes water and promotes dangerous algae growth. Algae blooms block sunlight and use up oxygen → harm fish.
Plant growth Photosynthesis is limited by sunlight (shading), CO₂, water, temperature, and soil nutrients. The resource in shortest supply sets the limit.
Weather & random events A late-spring cold front kills newborn offspring; a hurricane or flood washes away turtle nests and burrows. These are density-independent (next section).

Density-dependent vs. density-independent

The one-question test: Would this factor hurt a crowded population more (a bigger percentage) than a sparse one? Yes → density-dependent. No → density-independent.

Density-dependent Density-independent
Meaning Effect gets stronger as the population gets more crowded Affects the population the same way no matter its size or density — "not affected by population size" (your worksheet)
Usually Biotic Abiotic
Examples competition for food, water, space or mates; predation; disease & parasites (spread faster in crowds); waste buildup; nesting space; the bean-simulation rule "5 or more rabbits in a square die" weather (cold front, heat wave), drought, floods, hurricanes, wildfire, volcanic eruptions, some human actions (chemical spill, clear-cutting)

The #1 trap: "density-independent" does NOT mean "doesn't affect the population." A flood can kill 90% of a population! It means the percent affected doesn't depend on how crowded the population is.

Reading a data table: if the % killed rises as density rises → density-dependent. If the % killed stays the same at every density → density-independent.

Population growth & carrying capacity

Carrying capacity (K) = the largest population an area can support over time with its resources (food, water, land/space) without damaging the environment. A population usually stays near its carrying capacity because of the habitat's limiting factors.

Letter (class model) What it shows
A Slow start — few individuals to reproduce
B Exponential growth — plenty of resources; growth gets faster and faster
C Growth slows as limiting factors kick in
D Carrying capacity (K) — the dashed horizontal line
E, F, G, H Cyclical / periodic rise and fall around K (peaks E & G above; dips F & H below)

Carrying capacity can change:

Raises K (more can survive) Lowers K (fewer can survive)
more food, water, or shelter habitat destruction or fragmentation (development, roads)
more space or restored habitat drought, severe fire, pollution
fewer predators, competitors, or disease (e.g., humans hunting a rabbit's predators) overgrazing that damages the food supply
wildlife corridors reconnecting habitat new predators, competitors, invasive species, disease

Double-check this one: on your bean-simulation sheet, the "what could make K increase" and "what could make K decrease" answers were swapped (your teacher marked "swap"). Remember: more resources or fewer threats → higher K; habitat loss → lower K.

Overshoot, decline & recovery

From your worksheet: a growing population shows exponential growth and keeps growing until it overshoots the carrying capacity. It runs out of resources and declines rapidly until it can recover and stabilize around K.

Real example What happened
Australian rabbits (mid-1800s, worksheet) Plenty of vegetation + no predators → exponential growth → ate the land bare → crash (starvation) → vegetation recovered → rabbits increased again. A predator helped stabilize them.
St. Matthew Island reindeer (Alaska) 29 reindeer released in 1944, no predators → 1,350 (1957) → about 6,000 (1963) = overshoot; they overgrazed the lichen, then a brutal winter → only 42 left by 1966. Overgrazing lowered K permanently.

From your Carrying Capacity Simulation (beans = rabbits, paper grid = habitat):

Rule What it represents
5 or more rabbits in one square → remove all Overpopulation — not enough resources → they die (density-dependent)
Exactly 1 rabbit in a square → survives, no babies No mate → can't reproduce
2, 3, or 4 in a square → all become parents; add the same number of babies Reproduction
Year 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8
Rabbits 25 33 44 64 47 73 55 50 54
  • Years 0–3: rapid growth (positive relationship) — 25 → 64.
  • Years 3 and 5: overshoot (64, 73) followed by declines (47, 55).
  • Years 6–8: the population settles between 50 and 55 → K ≈ 50 rabbits.

Cyclical / periodic patterns

In the class explanatory model, after the population reaches K it keeps rising and falling above and below K (E–F–G–H). Your class labeled this the cyclical / periodic relationship. Why does it happen? When the population is above K, limiting factors (food shortage, competition, predators) push it down. When it is below K, resources recover and the population grows again.

Term (Ms. Hero's review notes) Meaning
Cyclical The pattern repeats, returning to the same state over and over (recurring cycles)
Periodic Changes happen at intervals, but may not return to exactly the same state

Where you'll see repeating patterns: populations fluctuating around K; predator–prey cycles (snowshoe hare and lynx, about every 10 years — see Topic 2); seasonal changes (populations rise in spring and summer, fall in winter).

On the test: if a graph shows a wavy, repeating up-and-down pattern, it is the cyclical/periodic part. In a predator–prey graph, the predator peaks come after the prey peaks.

Habitat fragmentation

Habitat fragmentation = breaking a large, continuous habitat into smaller, isolated patches (and often shrinking it), caused by human activities (roads, development, farming, logging) or natural causes like fire.

Why it matters:

  • Smaller, isolated populations → animals can't move to find food, mates, or new territory → less genetic variety.
  • Less interior habitat, more edge (edges are brighter, hotter, windier, and easier for predators and invasive species to reach).
  • Species that need large areas (pumas, interior-forest birds) decline first.
  • It disrupts the ecosystem's equilibrium and lowers carrying capacity.

Primary succession

Ecological succession = the gradual, fairly predictable change in the species living in an area over time.

Primary succession = building an ecosystem where there is NO soil — bare rock such as glacier-scraped granite (Yosemite), new lava flows, new volcanic islands, or rock exposed by a landslide.

Pioneer species = the very first organisms to colonize an area. In primary succession they are lichens (a fungus + alga mutualism) and mosses: tough, and they don't need soil. Lichens chemically and physically break rock into tiny pieces; their remains add organic matter → the first soil.

From "Ecological Succession: Life from Stone — How Yosemite was Made": Yosemite's granite formed deep underground from magma, was pushed up to the surface, and glaciers carved huge valleys and scraped the rock bare before melting away. That bare granite is where primary succession began.

What biodiversity is and why it matters

Biodiversity (biological diversity) = the variety of life — the variety of ecosystems, species, and genes in an area (or on Earth).

Why it matters: ecosystems with more biodiversity are usually more stable and resilient. If one species declines, others can fill its role (for example, a predator has other prey to switch to), so the food web doesn't collapse, and the ecosystem recovers faster after a disturbance.

How scientists measure it: they can't count every organism, so they sample — count individuals in sample plots (quadrats), along lines (transects), or from aerial counts (like the HHMI elephant census video) — and use the samples to estimate richness, evenness, and relative abundance.

Ecosystem

An ecosystem is all the organisms (the community) in an area interacting with each other and with the nonliving (abiotic) parts of their environment — sunlight, water, air, rocks, and soil. "Eco" means home: an ecosystem is the system where organisms live. Ecosystems come in different sizes — a pond, a forest, a rotting log, or the whole Earth.

Level What it includes Big Basin example

Community vs. ecosystem: a community is only the living things. An ecosystem adds the nonliving environment. If the answer choice mentions water, soil, or sunlight, it's describing an ecosystem.

Equilibrium and resilience

Equilibrium = a balance among the species in an ecosystem: populations rise and fall a little but stay within their normal ranges over time (a dynamic balance — like a population fluctuating around its carrying capacity).

Disrupts equilibrium Restores or protects equilibrium
removing a keystone species (wolves gone → deer 15,000 in Atlas NP) reintroducing the keystone species (wolves back → all populations normal)
habitat fragmentation wildlife corridors, protecting large habitat blocks
severe disturbances (high-severity fire, flood), drought, climate change succession; fire adaptations like redwood bark (resilience mechanisms)
pollution (waste → algae bloom), invasive species restoration by people (tertiary succession, prescribed fire)

Resilience = an ecosystem's ability to recover after a disturbance. Higher biodiversity usually means higher resilience.

Biotic vs. abiotic factors

Biotic (living or once living) Abiotic (nonliving)
plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, algae sunlight, temperature, water, air (O₂, CO₂)
dead leaves, fallen logs (once living) rocks, soil minerals, pH, wind
biotic interactions: competition, predation, disease, members of the same species competing for food weather and climate; abiotic "ecological forces or disturbances" such as drought and fire

Common traps

  • Dead things (a fallen log, dead leaves, a carcass) count as biotic in most class materials, because they were once living.
  • Soil is a mix: the minerals and water are abiotic; the bacteria, worms, and decaying matter (humus) are biotic.
  • Weather is abiotic (your Limiting Factors worksheet, Q10) — and it is also a density-independent limiting factor.

Worked CER 1 — Bean simulation. Question: What is the carrying capacity of rabbits in the habitat?

  • Claim: The carrying capacity of the habitat is about 50 rabbits.
  • Evidence: The population overshot to 64 rabbits in year 3 and 73 rabbits in year 5, then fell each time, and it stayed between 50 and 55 rabbits from year 6 to year 8.
  • Reasoning: When the population is above carrying capacity, limiting factors (crowding — 5 or more rabbits in a square die, a density-dependent factor) push it back down. A population that rises and falls around one level is fluctuating around its carrying capacity.

Worked CER 2 — Sampling Diversity. Question: Which community is more even, A or B?

  • Claim: Community B is more even than Community A.
  • Evidence: In Community B, every species makes up between 0.08 and 0.35 of the 100 individuals. In Community A, one species makes up 0.625 of the 200 individuals while another is only 0.015.
  • Reasoning: Evenness measures how similar the population sizes of the species are. B's species are all close to an equal share (0.20 each for 5 species), while A is dominated by a single species.

Worked CER 3 — Habitat fragmentation. Question: How does losing forest affect habitat for the marbled murrelet?

  • Claim: As more forest is burned or cleared, the habitat available to marbled murrelets drops — faster than the forest itself.
  • Evidence: When forest fell from 90 to 20 cells (−78%), suitable cells fell from 90 to 4 (−96%), and the habitat available fell from 100% to 20%.
  • Reasoning: Habitat fragmentation leaves small, isolated patches that are mostly edge. Species that need larger patches of intact forest can't use them, so usable habitat shrinks faster than total forest area.

Multiple-choice strategy for CER questions:

  • "Best claim?" → the one-sentence choice that answers exactly the question asked, with no data.
  • "Best evidence?" → specific, relevant numbers with units from the data given.
  • "Best reasoning?" → the choice that names a science concept and connects it to the evidence — not one that just restates the numbers.

4 Keystone vs. most abundant species A keystone species has an outsized effect; it can be rare. Atlas NP: 97 wolves vs. 7,000 deer — the wolves are keystone Keystone species
5 Which way food-web arrows point From the food → to the eater (the direction of energy flow). redwood sorrel → mule deer Food chains vs. food webs: reading the arrows
6 Autotroph vs. heterotroph Auto = self-feeder (makes its own food, producer); hetero = other-feeder (consumer or decomposer). redwood sorrel vs. brush rabbit Trophic levels: who eats whom
7 Decomposer vs. primary consumer Decomposers break down dead matter from every level; primary consumers eat living producers. fungi on a log vs. deer eating sorrel Decomposers, scavengers & detritivores
8 Where the "lost" 90% goes It is used for metabolism and released as heat — it isn't destroyed and isn't recycled. 10,000 kcal → 1,000 kcal Energy flow, metabolism & the 10% rule
9 Energy vs. matter Energy flows one way and leaves as heat; matter (nutrients) cycles. decomposers return nutrients to soil Decomposers, scavengers & detritivores
10 Omnivore's trophic level An omnivore can be at more than one level, depending on what it eats. black bear: level 2 eating sorrel, level 3 eating deer Trophic levels: who eats whom
11 Density-dependent vs. density-independent Ask: does crowding make the effect stronger? Yes → dependent (disease, competition). No → independent (flood, cold front, fire). disease in a crowded herd vs. a hurricane Density-dependent vs. density-independent
12 "Density-independent" = "no effect"? NO — it can kill many; the effect just doesn't depend on crowding. a flood killing 25% at any density Density-dependent vs. density-independent
13 Exponential vs. logistic growth Exponential (J) keeps getting steeper; logistic (S) levels off at K. bean years 0–3 vs. the whole class model Population growth & carrying capacity
14 What raises vs. lowers K More resources or fewer threats → K goes up. Habitat loss, drought, or overgrazing → K goes down. wildlife corridor (↑) vs. new subdivision (↓) Population growth & carrying capacity
15 Overshoot vs. carrying capacity Overshoot = the peak above the K line; K = the level the population settles around. bean sim: 73 (overshoot) vs. ~50 (K) Overshoot, decline & recovery
16 Predator vs. prey peaks Predator peaks come after prey peaks, and predators are fewer. lynx peaks lag hare peaks Cyclical / periodic patterns
17 Primary vs. secondary succession No soil → primary. Soil remains → secondary. A burned forest with soil is secondary. Yosemite granite vs. Big Basin after the fire Secondary & tertiary succession
18 Secondary vs. tertiary (Ms. Hero) Tertiary = soil lost and humans step in to restore. replanting an eroded, burned hillside Secondary & tertiary succession
19 Pioneer vs. climax Pioneer = first to arrive (lichens; grasses); climax = final, stable community. lichen on rock vs. old-growth redwoods Who lives there: pioneers, climax community & more
20 Crown vs. surface fire Crown = burns the treetops, kills most trees. Surface = burns the forest floor, big trees survive. fire jumping tree to tree vs. burning needles and logs Fire: surface vs. crown, severity & disturbances
21 Fire severity vs. intensity Severity = the ecosystem damage (effects); intensity = the heat released (behavior). 77% high-severity burn in Big Basin Fire: surface vs. crown, severity & disturbances
22 Fragmentation: amount vs. pattern The same total forest can be very different habitat depending on how it's broken up. checkerboard vs. two big blocks (both 50% forest) Habitat fragmentation
23 Richness vs. evenness Richness = number of species; evenness = how equal their counts are. Field X (even, 2 species) vs. Field Y (rich, uneven) Richness and evenness
24 Relative abundance formula (individuals of ONE species) ÷ (total individuals) — not number of species ÷ total. 125 ÷ 200 = 0.625 Relative abundance (with your class data)
25 Community vs. ecosystem Community = living things only; ecosystem = community + abiotic factors. all Big Basin species vs. species + creek + sunlight Ecosystem
26 Biotic vs. abiotic (dead things) Once-living things (logs, dead leaves) are biotic; rocks, water, sunlight, and weather are abiotic. fallen redwood log = biotic Biotic vs. abiotic factors
27 IV vs. DV (DRY MIX) IV = what you change (x-axis); DV = what you measure (y-axis). Time → x-axis. years (x) vs. rabbit population (y) DRY MIX: axes, IV & DV
28 Line vs. bar graph Line = change over time; bar = separate categories. bean data → line graph Choosing the right graph
29 Correlation vs. cause A trend on a graph doesn't prove what caused it. more fires over time ≠ proof of one cause Reading and interpreting graphs
30 Evidence vs. reasoning Evidence = the data (with units); reasoning = the science concept explaining why the data supports the claim. "deer fell to 7,000" vs. "wolves are a keystone predator…" Claim, evidence, reasoning
