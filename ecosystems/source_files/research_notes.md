# ECOSYSTEMS Unit - Research Notes (Ms. Hero, 25-Q MC test)
Compiled 2026-10-25. **[!]** = sources disagree / caution.

## 1. Earth Systems
- **Atmosphere** = air/gases. **Hydrosphere** = all water, any state: ocean (97% salty), rivers, groundwater, vapor, **ice**. **Biosphere** = all living things. **Geosphere/lithosphere** = rock, soil, sediment, crust to core.
- [!] In the HS 4-sphere model, ice goes in the hydrosphere ("cryosphere" is a subset). NatGeo lists the cryosphere as a 5th system. HS materials use geosphere and lithosphere interchangeably.
- Interactions run both ways and chain together (NOAA):
  - **Volcano**: ash, SO2 and CO2 go into the air, causing acid rain, which acidifies soil and lakes and harms living things. Lava can melt glaciers, causing mudflows.
  - **Photosynthesis**: uses CO2 (atmo) + water (hydro) + minerals (geo).
  - **Hurricane**: warm ocean fuels the storm, which erodes coasts and destroys habitat.
  - **Weathering**: rain + roots/lichen break down rock.
  - **Rain → erosion**: sediment washes into streams.
  - **Respiration**: returns CO2 to the air.
- **Wildfire touches all four**:
  - Bio: vegetation is the fuel; organisms die.
  - Atmo: lightning and wind start and drive it; it releases smoke, CO2 and heat.
  - Geo: burns the soil organic layer; can make soil water-repellent; erosion follows.
  - Hydro: drought sets it up; afterward runoff rises and ash/sediment reach streams.

Sources: https://gml.noaa.gov/education/info_activities/pdfs/TBI_earth_spheres.pdf ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/earths-systems/

## 2. Ecological Relationships
- **Symbiosis** = close, long-term interaction between two species. [!] NatGeo lists 4 types (including competition). Most HS texts list 3; predation is separate.
- **Mutualism (+/+)**: clownfish/anemone; **lichen** (fungus + alga or cyanobacteria); **mycorrhizae** (fungi trade water and minerals for root sugars); cleaner wrasse/fish; bees/flowers; coral/algae.
- **Commensalism (+/0)**: barnacles on whales; remora/shark; cattle egret/cattle; orchids on trees.
- **Parasitism (+/-)**, host usually not killed quickly: ticks, fleas (outside the host); tapeworm (inside); mistletoe (a *hemi*parasite that still photosynthesizes); cuckoo laying eggs in other birds' nests.
- [!] **Oxpecker**: classically a mutualism (eats ticks), but it also keeps wounds open and drinks blood, so it is now called "facultative" or partly parasitic. Not a clean example.
- **Predation (+/-)**: lynx/hare, wolf/elk, otter/urchin.
- **Competition (-/-)**: **intraspecific** = same species; **interspecific** = different species (sponges vs. corals).
- **Keystone species**: its effect is far larger than its abundance. Removing it changes the ecosystem.
  - *Pisaster* sea star (Paine, Washington): when removed, mussels crowded out other species. [!] Experiment published 1966; the term "keystone" coined in 1969.
  - Sea otters: eat urchins, protecting kelp forests.
  - Yellowstone wolves: reintroduced 1995–96.
  - Beavers: "ecosystem engineers."
  - Pollinators: sometimes called keystone mutualists.
- **Trophic cascade**: a change at the top ripples down several levels (otters up → urchins down → kelp up). [!] NPS calls the Yellowstone wolf cascade debated; cougars, hunting and water also matter.

Sources: https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/symbiosis-art-living-together/ ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2010.01212.x ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-big-scientific-debate-trophic-cascades.htm

## 3. Food Chains, Webs and Energy
- **Autotroph/producer**: makes its own food (plants, algae, phytoplankton). **Heterotroph/consumer**: eats other organisms.
- **Trophic levels**: 1 producer; 2 primary consumer (herbivore); 3 secondary; 4 tertiary; 5 quaternary or apex.
  - NatGeo chain: grass → grasshopper → mouse → snake → hawk.
  - Omnivores can sit at several levels. Most ecosystems have about 5 levels at most.
- **Decomposers** (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter chemically. **Detritivores** (earthworms, millipedes) *eat* detritus. **Scavengers** (vultures) eat carcasses.
  - [!] NatGeo and many classes lump these together as "decomposers."
  - Decomposers feed on *all* levels and are usually left off food webs (Khan).
- **Arrows point from the eaten to the eater** = the direction energy flows.
- **Energy flows one way** and ends up as heat. **Matter cycles.**
- **10% rule**: about 10% of energy passes to the next level. The other ~90% (NatGeo):
  1. Released as heat by cellular respiration and metabolism.
  2. Lost as undigested waste.
  3. Never eaten (dead parts go to decomposers).
- **Metabolism** = sum of all chemical reactions in an organism. Respiration (glucose + O2 → CO2 + H2O + ATP) always loses some energy as heat.
- **Worked example**: 10,000 kcal → 1,000 → 100 → 10 → 1. Going up, ÷10; going down, ×10.
- **Pyramids** (OpenStax): energy pyramids are always upright. Biomass pyramids can invert (ocean phytoplankton). Number pyramids can invert (one tree, many insects).

Sources: https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/energy-flow-and-10-percent-rule/ ; https://www.khanacademy.org/science/high-school-biology/hs-ecology/trophic-levels/a/food-chains-and-food-webs-article ; https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/46-2-energy-flow-through-ecosystems

## 4. Population Dynamics
- **Limiting factor**: anything that keeps a population from growing larger.
- **Density-dependent**: stronger as crowding rises; mostly biotic. Competition, predation, disease, parasitism, waste buildup, living space.
- **Density-independent**: acts *regardless of size or density*; mostly abiotic. Weather, drought, flood, fire, volcano, pollution.
- [!] **Ambiguous cases**: CK-12 calls habitat destruction density-independent; the Rabbit Gizmo treats less land as density-dependent (it lowers K). Test: "Does the % affected depend on crowding?"
- **Exponential growth (J-curve)** vs. **logistic (S-curve)**: logistic levels off at **carrying capacity (K)**, the maximum the environment can support long-term.
- **Overshoot** above K damages resources, then comes a **die-off/crash**. The population may recover (often to a lower K) or go extinct. **Boom-and-bust** = repeated rises and crashes.
- **St. Matthew Island reindeer (VERIFIED)**:
  - Aug 1944: **29** released by the Coast Guard; no predators.
  - 1957: **1,350**.
  - 1963: **~6,000** (about 47 per square mile; lichen overgrazed).
  - Extreme winter of 1963–64 caused mass starvation.
  - 1966: **42** left (41 females, 1 likely sterile male), a ~99% crash; extinct by the 1980s.
  - Cause: food supply interacting with climate (Klein 1968). [!] Later work stresses the weather more.
- **Lynx and snowshoe hare** (Hudson's Bay Company pelt records, about 1845–1930s, compiled by Elton):
  - **~10-year cycle** (average 9.6 years; range about 9–11).
  - **Lynx peaks lag behind hare peaks.** [!] Lag estimates range from about 1 to 4 years.
  - [!] Hares also cycle where there are no lynx. Kluane (Yukon) studies point to predation by *many* predators, plus food.
- **Kaibab deer**: predators removed 1906–31; claimed irruption of 30,000–100,000, then a crash. [!] Caughley (1970) showed the numbers were unreliable and livestock were removed at the same time. Textbooks dropped it; treat it as a cautionary tale.

Sources: https://el-gizmos.s3.amazonaws.com/grizzly/materials/RabbitPopulationSeasonVocab.pdf ; https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/when-reindeer-paradise-turned-purgatory-0 ; https://www.mun.ca/biology/scarr/Hudson_Bay_population_cycles.html ; https://besjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1365-2656.12720 ; https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/science/kaibab-plateau-deer-disaster/

## 5. Cyclical vs. Periodic
- **Cyclical** = a repeating rise and fall; each cycle's length and height can vary (hare-lynx, ~8–11 years).
- **Periodic** = repeats at a **fixed, regular interval** (seasons, day/night, tides).
- Time-series statistics uses the same split: "seasonal" means a fixed period; "cyclic" means not fixed. Its textbook example is the lynx data, described as "aperiodic cycles of ~10 years" (Hyndman/FPP).
- The Rabbit Gizmo population rises in spring/summer and falls in winter every year, so it is periodic (seasonal).
- [!] Many sources use the words interchangeably, and no HS standard defines the difference. Rule of thumb: all periodic patterns are cyclical, but not all cyclical patterns are periodic.

Sources: https://otexts.com/fpp2/tspatterns.html ; https://robjhyndman.com/hyndsight/cyclicts/

## 6. Succession
- **Primary**: **no soil** to start (new lava, retreating glacier, dunes, landslides).
  - **Pioneers** are lichens and mosses. Lichen acids break rock down **chemically**; growth into cracks breaks it **physically**.
  - Organic matter builds up into soil, then come mosses and ferns, then grasses, shrubs and trees.
- **Secondary**: **soil remains** (after fire, flood, logging, or on abandoned farmland). Pioneers are grasses and weeds. It is **faster**.
- **Timescales**: primary takes centuries to thousands of years (USGS: wet Hawaiian lava can grow a forest in under 150 years). Secondary takes decades to about 100–200 years.
- **Climax community**: a relatively stable endpoint (e.g., coast redwood forest) that lasts until the next disturbance. [!] Modern ecologists see it as dynamic.
- **Disturbances**: natural (fire, flood, volcano, storms) and human (logging, farming, development, invasive species).
- **"Tertiary succession"**: [!] **not a standard term**. Proposed by Rapson (2023, *Restoration Ecology*) for human-led restoration; disputed by Prach (2023). Ms. Hero's definition fits Rapson; use hers.
- I could not find the "Life from Stone" lesson online.

Sources: https://news.uchicago.edu/explainer/what-is-ecological-succession ; https://www.usgs.gov/news/volcano-watch-lava-flow-forest-primary-succession ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/rec.13683 ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/rec.13892

## 7. Wildfire Ecology
- **Types of fire**:
  - **Ground fire**: smolders below the litter (duff, roots).
  - **Surface fire**: burns the forest floor; most big trees survive.
  - **Crown fire**: burns through the treetops; the most destructive.
- **Ladder fuels**: shrubs, saplings and low branches that carry fire into the crowns.
- **Intensity vs. severity**:
  - **Intensity** = energy or heat released (fire behavior).
  - **Severity** = effect on the ecosystem: loss of organic matter above and below ground. Rated low/moderate/high.
  - High intensity usually, not always, means high severity (Keeley).
- **High severity effects**: duff and seed bank burn off ("sterilized" soil), a **hydrophobic** layer can form, and runoff, erosion and debris flows follow.
- **Coast redwood** (NPS):
  - Bark about **12 in thick**, with no resin.
  - Tannins: [!] mainly protect against insects and fungi; one NPS page also credits fire resistance.
  - Resprouts from **burls** and dormant **epicormic buds**.
- **Knobcone pine**: **serotinous** cones sealed with resin that open in fire. [!] USFS says ~397 °F on average; CalMatters says 350 °F or more.

Sources: https://www.nps.gov/articles/wildland-fire-spread-and-suppression.htm ; https://www.usgs.gov/publications/fire-intensity-fire-severity-and-burn-severity-a-brief-review-and-suggested-usage ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/coast-redwood.htm ; https://research.fs.usda.gov/feis/species-reviews/pinatt

## 8. CZU Lightning Complex 2020 (CAL FIRE, VERIFIED)
- **Started Aug 16, 2020** from a dry-lightning storm. **Contained Sept 22** (37 days).
- **86,509 acres**, Santa Cruz and San Mateo counties.
- **1,490 structures destroyed**, 140 damaged, **1 death**.
- [!] Other counts: Potter/WERT says 1,450 structures; the SF Chronicle reported 925 homes. Lightning-strike counts vary from 11,000 to 15,000.
- **Big Basin Redwoods State Park** (**1902**, California's oldest state park):
  - **More than 97% burned.** In the park: 77% high, 20% moderate, 2% low severity.
  - Nearly all infrastructure destroyed.
  - Limited reopening reported for July 2022.
- **Old-growth survival**: [!] **I found no official count.** State Parks (2021) said "most of the redwoods survived," and the Mother and Father of the Forest still stand. A satellite study (Potter 2023) found only 24% of the canopy regrowing by 2022, made worse by drought. Douglas-fir mortality was about 100%.
- **Recovery**:
  - Sprouts appeared within about 8 months.
  - *Nature Plants* (2023): they grew from buds dormant for **centuries**, fueled by carbon reserves more than 50 years old.
  - Knobcone pines reseeded.
- **Climate**: record heat wave, dry fuels and rare lightning. The fire was the most intense seen in most redwood groves in at least 100 years. [!] The role of past logging and fire suppression versus extreme weather is debated.

Sources: https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2020/8/16/czu-lightning-complex-including-warnella-fire ; https://journal.wildlife.ca.gov/2023/04/12/impacts-of-the-czu-lightning-complex-fire-of-august-2020-on-the-forests-of-big-basin-redwoods-state-park/ ; https://www.savetheredwoods.org/newsroom/press-releases/research-reveals-how-old-growth-coast-redwoods-survive-wildfire/ ; https://calmatters.org/environment/2021/04/big-basin-park-heals-wildfires/

## 9. Habitat Fragmentation
- **Definition**: continuous habitat broken into small, isolated patches. **Causes**: roads, development, farming, logging, dams, fire.
- **Effects**: inbreeding and **less gene flow**; **edge effects** (more light, wind and heat, less humidity, more invasive species); roadkill; wide-ranging species decline.
- **Mitigation**: corridors, overpasses and underpasses (with fencing), conservation easements.
- **Wallis Annenberg Wildlife Crossing** (US-101, Agoura Hills):
  - Vegetated bridge about **210 × 174 ft**, about **$92M**; Caltrans calls it the "largest of its type."
  - Construction started 2022; bridge finished June 2025.
  - **Ribbon cutting scheduled Dec 2, 2026, so NOT yet open.**
- **Why it was built** (NPS): Santa Monica Mountains lions have near-record low genetic diversity, with kinked tails seen. Modeling: **99.7% chance of extinction in 50 years** if isolated, but **2.4%** if one new lion arrives every 2 years. P-22 is the campaign icon.
- **Hwy 17 Laurel Curve undercrossing**:
  - Built by Caltrans, the Santa Cruz County Regional Transportation Commission and the Land Trust of Santa Cruz County; **completed about Jan 2023**.
  - First lion on camera Nov 2023; about 934 passages in the first year.
  - [!] Cost: $5.4M construction contract vs. ~$12M total cited.

Sources: https://dot.ca.gov/caltrans-near-me/district-7/district-7-projects/d7-101-annenberg-wildlife-crossing ; https://spectrumlocalnews.com/ca/california/human-interest/2026/10/09/barring-heavy-el-ni-o-rain--wallis-annenberg-wildlife-crossing-set-to-open-december-2nd ; https://www.nps.gov/articles/mountain-lions-face-loss-of-genetic-diversity.htm ; https://www.sccrtc.org/construction-begins-on-highway-17-wildlife-undercrossing-at-laurel-curve/

## 10. Biodiversity
- **Richness** = number of species. **Evenness** = how equal their abundances are.
- **Relative abundance** = (individuals of ONE species ÷ total individuals) × 100%. (The top is individuals of one species, not the number of species.)
- **Worked example** (richness 4, N = 100 in both):
  - A = 25/25/25/25 (25% each).
  - B = 85/5/5/5.
  - A is **more diverse**. Simpson's index (1 − Σp²): A = 0.75, B ≈ 0.27. Probably beyond the test.
- More diverse communities are more resilient.
- **Sampling**: quadrats; transects; **mark-recapture**: N ≈ (marked × 2nd catch) ÷ recaptured marked (40 × 50 ÷ 10 = 200).

Source: https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/sites/default/files/topic/Research/lessons_MeasuringBiodiversityEducatorHandout.pdf

## 11. Ecosystems, Biotic vs. Abiotic
- **Ecosystem** = community + nonliving environment, interacting. Levels: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere.
- **Biotic** = living or once-living things and their products (dead leaves, logs, feces).
- **Abiotic** = sunlight, temperature, water, soil minerals, pH, rocks, gases.
- [!] **Trap**: most HS worksheets count dead leaves and logs as **biotic**; NatGeo says biotic = only "living." Use the class definition. Soil is a mix.
- **Stability** (NGSS HS-LS2-6):
  - **Dynamic equilibrium**: numbers and types of organisms stay relatively constant under stable conditions.
  - **Resilience**: recovering after a modest disturbance.
  - Extreme change can produce a new ecosystem.
  - "Homeostasis" really applies to organisms.

Sources: https://www.nextgenscience.org/dci-arrangement/hs-ls2-ecosystems-interactions-energy-and-dynamics ; https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/ecosystem/

## 12. Graphing
- **DRY MIX**: Dependent/Responding → Y-axis; Manipulated/Independent → X-axis.
- **IV** is deliberately changed; **DV** is measured; **constants** stay the same; the **control group** is the untreated baseline. Gizmo example: IV = land, DV = carrying capacity.
- Graph types: **line** for change over time; **bar** for categories; **pie** for parts of a whole. Always label axes with units.

Source: https://www.mnemonic-device.com/arithmetic/dry-mix/

## 13. CER (Claim-Evidence-Reasoning)
- **Claim**: a one-sentence answer to the question.
- **Evidence**: data that is **appropriate** (relevant) and **sufficient**, quantitative (with units) or qualitative. Not opinion.
- **Reasoning**: links the evidence to the claim through a **scientific principle** (McNeill & Krajcik).
- **Example**:
  - *Claim*: Food shortage plus a severe winter crashed the St. Matthew reindeer.
  - *Evidence*: They went from 29 to ~6,000 with no predators; lichen was overgrazed; only 42 were left by 1966.
  - *Reasoning*: Exponential growth overshot carrying capacity; overgrazing lowered K; density-dependent starvation plus density-independent weather caused the crash.

Source: https://dpi.wi.gov/sites/default/files/imce/science/pdf/mcneill-krajcik-nsta-cer-inquiry-2008.pdf

## Likely Test Traps
1. Food-web arrows point **toward the eater** (the direction energy flows).
2. Energy flows one way and ends as heat; matter cycles. Energy pyramids never invert.
3. The "lost" 90% is mostly **heat from respiration**, plus waste.
4. Going up a level: ÷10. Going down: ×10. Producers are level 1.
5. Decomposers are **not** primary consumers; they feed on all levels.
6. Density-independent = the effect doesn't depend on crowding. It does not mean "doesn't affect the population."
7. Disease and competition are density-dependent. Weather and fire are density-independent.
8. Carrying capacity can **drop** (overgrazing, habitat loss).
9. Overshoot leads to a crash, not a smooth S-curve.
10. Predator peaks come **after** prey peaks.
11. Commensalism (+/0) vs. mutualism (+/+).
12. Parasites rarely kill quickly; predators kill.
13. Lichen = a **mutualism** and the classic pioneer species.
14. Fire where soil remains = **secondary** succession.
15. Crown fire (treetops) vs. surface fire (floor) vs. ground fire (below the surface).
16. Intensity = heat released; severity = ecosystem damage.
17. Same richness does not mean same diversity; evenness matters.
18. A keystone species is not the most abundant species.
19. Dead leaves/logs are usually **biotic**.
20. Periodic = fixed interval; cyclical = can vary. The IV goes on the x-axis.
