## B. Teacher's review-session summary (Ecology Key Concepts.pdf)
- Ecosystem = system where producers, consumers and species interact (food webs, energy). Interdependence: primary consumers depend on producers, secondary on primary; disruptions ripple through system. Balance disrupted by habitat fragmentation & limiting factors; **resilience mechanisms** restore function.
- Earth system science: hydrosphere & biosphere related to carrying capacity; climate change → fires as Earth-system process.
- **Primary succession**: from area with **no soil**. **Lichen** attaches to bare rock, chemically/physically weathers it into tiny pieces → soil. Linked to post-disturbance where areas completely burned may begin anew with pioneers like lichen.
- **Secondary succession**: after disturbance that **leaves soil intact** (e.g., regrowth after fire); faster than primary.
- **Tertiary succession** (teacher's term): soil NOT intact and **humans intervene** to restore the ecosystem (human-led restoration when natural recovery is insufficient due to severe soil loss/degradation). NOTE: not a standard textbook term — use teacher's definition.
- Richness = count of distinct species. Evenness = how evenly individuals are distributed across species (high evenness = similar population sizes; low = one/few dominate). Relative abundance = a species' proportion of all individuals.
- Mutualism (+/+), Parasitism (+/−), Commensalism (+/0).
- Interspecific competition = between different species (ex: lion and tiger competing for water buffalo). Intraspecific = within same species (ex: seagulls competing for nesting space on a rock).
- ~10% of energy transferred to the next trophic level.
- Keystone species: removal can cause ecosystem collapse. Ex: **bees** (pollination → plants → primary consumers → cascade).
- Primary consumers eat producers (ex: **deer**). Secondary consumers depend on primary consumers.
- Carrying capacity: maximum population an ecosystem can sustain with available resources without environmental degradation.
- Limiting factors: restrict growth; biotic or abiotic; incl. resource scarcity, habitat fragmentation, fires influenced by climate change. Nature counters via resilience (fire-resistant traits; primary succession).
- Habitat fragmentation: breaking up / reduction of habitat size and continuity due to human activities or natural causes (e.g., **fires**); disrupts equilibrium; harms species needing specific habitat sizes.
- Fire resilience: **coast redwoods have very thick bark** and insulation; lichen post-fire soil formation.
- Biotic = living (ex: members of same species competing for food). Abiotic = nonliving (rocks, water).
- **Cyclical**: repeats back to the same state/pattern over time (recurring cycles). **Periodic**: changes occur at intervals but may not return to the exact same state. (Teacher's framing — use this. Note: the carrying-capacity explanatory model labels the wavy part near K as "Cyclical / Periodic Relationship" — i.e., the oscillation of a population around K.)

### C3. Limiting Factors and Carrying Capacity worksheet
- "A **limiting factor** is an environmental factor that causes a population to decrease [stop growing]." Examples: food & water, space, weather. Limiting factors can be biotic or abiotic (or both).
- **Bear example**: a bear must eat 10 fish/day; river provides ~100 fish/day without harming fish population → max **10 bears** (5 bears need 50 fish; 15 bears wouldn't all survive). "No matter how much shelter and water there was, the population would not get larger than 10 bears."
- Plants: photosynthesis limited by sunlight (shading by taller plants), CO2, temperature, water, soil nutrients.
- Animal waste → water pollution → dangerous **algae** growth → risk to fish.
- **Living space**: gulls nest on rocky shores; if a pair can't find nesting room, no offspring. Plants: crowded roots run out of space; branches shade small plants.
- **Weather & random events**: "Weather conditions such as temperature and the amount of rainfall can also limit population growth. A cold front that comes in late spring can kill the offspring of many species... A hurricane or flood can wash away turtle nests and burrows. These factors are **density-independent** factors because they are **not affected by population size**."
- **Carrying capacity**: "The largest population an area can support with its resources (food, water, land)... A population usually stays near its carrying capacity because of the limiting factors of a habitat." Growth begins **exponential**, continues until population **overshoots** carrying capacity, runs out of resources and **declines** rapidly until it can **recover** and stabilize around K.
- **Australia rabbits** (mid-1800s): plenty of vegetation, no predators → rapid increase → ate land bare → crashed (starved) → vegetation recovered → rabbits increased again. "The reintroduction of a predator, the lynx, has helped stabilize the population." (worksheet's framing)
- Graph labeling: exponential growth (steep rise), overshoot (first peak above dashed K line), decline (fall after peak), recovery (rise from trough), carrying capacity (dashed horizontal line).

### C5. Gizmos: Ecosystems STEM Case — HANDBOOK
- Ecosystems = many living things in one environment + nonliving things (sunlight, water, air, rocks, soil). Healthy ecosystems have a balance, or **equilibrium**, between all species. "Eco" means **home**. Ecosystems can be **different sizes** (ponds, forests, the Earth).
- All organisms need **energy**. Energy transferred as species eat each other → **food chain**. **Arrows point from the food to the organism eating the food** = direction of energy flow. Each level = **trophic level** ("food level").
- First source of energy = **sunlight**. Plants convert light energy to chemical energy (sugars) by photosynthesis → **producers**, **first trophic level**, also called **autotrophs** (auto = self, troph = food).
- Primary consumers (primary = first; consume = eat) eat producers. Secondary consumers eat primary consumers. Tertiary consumers eat secondary (and may also eat primary).
- **Heterotrophs** (hetero = other) get food from other organisms. **Herbivores** eat only producers (ex: deer). **Omnivores** eat producers and consumers (ex: bears). (Carnivores eat only animals.)
- Not all energy is transferred: all organisms have **metabolic processes** that need energy. **During metabolism, some chemical energy is converted to heat energy; heat leaves the organism, goes into the environment, and leaves the food chain.** Producers have the most energy. 1,000 calories = 1 kilocalorie (kcal). Producers 10,000 kcal → 10% → primary consumers get **1,000 kcal**.
- "Cycling of energy": when organisms die they are eaten by **scavengers** (animals that feed on dead animals, e.g., vultures eat a dead raccoon) and broken up by **decomposers** (fungi, bacteria use **enzymes** to break down dead organisms). Organisms that survive on dead/decaying organisms = **detritivores**. Decomposition returns nutrients to the soil; nutrients taken up by **producers**.
- Food web = many food chains combined; a healthy real-world food web is very complicated & diverse.
- **Keystone species**: more important than others; if removed, can destroy the balance. There can be **more than one** keystone species in an ecosystem.
- **CASE STUDY — Atlas National Park** (Gizmo): populations out of balance. Normal ranges vs. now:
  | Species | Normal range | Now |
  |---|---|---|
  | Grass | 800–1000 blades/m² | 400 |
  | Coyotes | 250–300 | 600 |
  | Ravens | 220–270 | 160 |
  | Deer | 4000–8000 | 15000 |
  | Bees | 4000–5000 hives | 2000 |
  | Wolves | 95–120 | 0 |
  - Adding **bees** → after 5 yrs: grass 350, coyotes 650, ravens 145, deer 17000, bees 3000, wolves 0 → NOT fixed (worse).
  - Adding **wolves** → after 5 yrs: grass 850, coyotes 270, ravens 245, deer 7000, bees 4250, wolves 97 → all normal (ravens high-normal).
  - Conclusion: **wolves are the keystone species** in Atlas NP (wolves were the first species to drop out of normal range, ~1920; their loss let deer & coyotes boom, deer overgrazed grass, which hurt bees, etc.). People might say bees (pollinators are important) — but data show reintroducing wolves restored **equilibrium**. (Analogous to real Yellowstone wolves, 1995–96.)

### C6. Ecological Succession: Life from Stone — How Yosemite was Made (10/22/26)
- Yosemite: granite formed deep underground from magma, pushed to surface by tectonic plates; **glaciers** carved huge valleys and melted away, leaving bare granite.
- Primary succession: slow process of building an ecosystem where **no soil** is found; occurs on bare surfaces like volcanic rock or glacial granite.
- Pioneer species: the very first organisms to arrive in an ecosystem; tough, don't require soil; **break rock down into tiny pieces → foundation of soil**.
- Climax community: the final (mature, relatively stable) form of a developed ecosystem.
- **Primary Succession Timeline** (class table):
  | Stage | Pioneer | Early Intermediate | Late Intermediate | Climax Community |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Years | 0–150 | 150–500 | 500–1000 | 1000+ |
  | Organisms | lichen, crevice-dwelling plants | grasses & wildflowers, primary consumers | shrubs & small trees, secondary consumers | large trees, tertiary consumers, apex predators |
  | Soil | tiny rocks, not soil yet | gravelly, shallow soil | moderate depth & quality | deep, rich soil |
  | Light at ground | maximum | slightly less | moderate (shade from small trees) | little (shade kills sun-loving species) |
  | Impact | break bare rock into tiny pieces → foundation of soil | add organic material, nitrogen (fertilizer), stabilize soil | add nitrogen, trap moisture, decomposers speed breakdown | blocks sunlight; diversifies organisms |
- Climax community residents marked: **Residents** (star; persist in climax: large trees, apex predators), **Edge-Dwellers** (check; e.g., grasses/shrubs at edges), **Retired Pioneers** (X; lichen, crevice plants largely replaced).
- **Disturbance card: Crown Fire or Surface Fire** (student had **surface fire**): surface fire had little to no effect on the upper tree canopy; cleared dry organic matter on forest floor (dry needles, fallen branches); mostly positive — cleared dead matter, left room for seeds, soil stays healthy; tall canopies largely untouched; killed low-dwelling organisms (small mammals, reptiles); seeds stored in soil (seed bank) begin to grow.
  - Crown fire (standard): burns through the tree canopy (tops), spreads tree-to-tree, kills most trees, most destructive/high severity; often fed by **ladder fuels**.
- **Secondary succession**: process of an established ecosystem recovering from a disturbance (soil intact).
- **Secondary Succession Timeline** (class table):
  | Stage | Immediate aftermath | Early recovery (pioneers) | Mid-recovery (intermediate) | Long-term recovery (climax) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Years | 0–1 | 1–20 | 20–150 | 150+ |
  | Organisms | what remains: large trees, tertiary consumers, some primary/secondary consumers, apex predators | grasses & wildflowers (who first moved back in) | shrubs & small trees | fully recovered |
  | Soil | remains healthy & moist | healthy | healthy | healthy |
  | Light | slightly more than before fire | ~same | a little less (trees growing) | less than before |
  | Impact | clears dead organic matter; stored seeds grow | new trees starting to sprout | | |

### C7. Sampling Diversity (10/13/26)
- Biodiversity = variety of life on Earth (ecosystems, organisms, their habitats and genes). Scientists sample to estimate species abundance; a community's biodiversity is often measured with **relative abundances** of species gathered through sampling. Relative abundances = estimate of actual quantities of species relative to others in the same area. HHMI elephant census video (sampling).
- Biodiversity measured with **richness** (number of different species in an area) and **evenness** (how similar the numbers of each species are). Rainforests are very rich. A **grass lawn is very uneven** (grass far outnumbers clovers, dandelions, ants, bees). Even = all species have equal counts.

## D. Carrying Capacity Simulations.pdf (8 pp.)

### D1. Carrying Capacity Simulation Activity (bean "rabbits" on a paper grid habitat, 10/16)
- Start with 25 beans (rabbits). Drop on grid. Beans touching a line → move into square where most of the bean lies.
  - **≥5 rabbits in a square → remove all** (overpopulation; not enough resources → died).
  - **Exactly 1 in a square → survives but does not reproduce** (no mate).
  - **2, 3, or 4 in a square → all become parents; add the same number of babies.**
- Repeat 8 rounds ("years"). Graph **Population of rabbits on y-axis, Time (years) on x-axis**. Estimate K and label it.
- Student data (years 0–8): **25, 33, 44, 64, 47, 73, 55, 50, 54**. Student estimated **K ≈ 50 rabbits**.
- Teacher feedback: student drew a **bar graph — "Wrong type of graph"** → should be a **LINE graph** (continuous change over time). (Key lesson!)
- Analysis prompts: "From __ to __, the graph shows a ___ (positive/negative) relationship between ___ and ___." Years 0→3 positive (25→64); years 5→6 negative (73→55).
- Fluctuations in real world: predator numbers change, food, disease, weather.
- What increases K: more food/water/shelter/space, fewer predators (e.g., humans hunting predators), habitat restoration. What decreases K: habitat destruction/fragmentation by humans, drought, fire, more predators/competitors. (Student swapped the two answers → teacher marked "swap" → likely confusion.)

### D2. Carrying Capacity Explanatory Model (graph with letters)
- Population size (y) vs Time / Number of generations (x). Points: **A** = start, slow growth (lag) at bottom-left; **B** = steep rise = **exponential relationship**; **C** = curve bending/slowing as it approaches K; **D** = dashed horizontal line labeled at the left = **carrying capacity**; **E** and **G** = small peaks just above the dashed line; **F** and **H** = small dips just below → the wavy portion E–F–G–H = **cyclical/periodic relationship** (population fluctuates around K). Explanation: population increases until it reaches K, then stops increasing/decreasing dramatically and fluctuates around K.

### D3. Habitat Fragmentation Simulation (10/20/26)
- Different bird species use different habitat parts: some prefer open habitats created by death of a tree, fire, or harvesting; some prefer closed canopy; some require **large areas** for hunting/finding mates. Affected by **amount AND pattern** of landscape change.
- 10×10 grid = 100 cells. **Gray = forest (canopy intact); White = meadow (previously burned area, now open space).**
- Exercise 1: as % meadow rises 10, 20, 40, 60, 80 (forest cells left 90, 80, 60, 40, 20), count suitable cells for species; % habitat available = suitable ÷ forest cells × 100 (for Say's Phoebe, meadow-based: suitable ÷ meadow cells).
  - Species 1 **Pileated Woodpecker**; 2 **Marbled Murrelet** (student's species); 3 **Hermit Thrush**; 4 **Northern Saw-Whet Owl**; 5 **Say's Phoebe** (meadow/open-space species).
  - Student Marbled Murrelet data: suitable cells 90, 79, 55, 24, 4 → % available 100%, 98.8%, 91.7%, 60%, 20%. → As forest is cleared, suitable habitat declines faster than forest area itself (fragmentation effect).
- Exercise 2: 50% forest burned, 6 spatial patterns: **A Checkerboard, B Random, C Stripes, D Four Areas (two 5×5 forest blocks on a diagonal + two meadow blocks), E Diamond (one central diamond of forest), F Two Areas (forest half + meadow half)**. Same total forest (50 cells) but different arrangements → different habitat availability. Checkerboard = most fragmented (no forest cells share sides; all edge). Two Areas/Four Areas/Diamond keep big contiguous blocks → best for interior-forest species needing large areas. Student's Marbled Murrelet ranking: Stripes 1, Four Areas 2, Diamond 3, Two Areas 4, Random 5, Checkerboard 6.
- Recommendation: manage landscape to accommodate all species → a mix (some large intact forest blocks + some open meadow + edges); use evidence and reasoning (CER).
- Key concept: **habitat fragmentation** = large continuous habitat broken into smaller, isolated patches; more **edge**, less **interior**; species needing large interior areas decline even if total forest area is the same.

## G. Standard HS facts to use (see research_notes.md for sources)
- Wildfire touches all 4 spheres; volcano, hurricane, photosynthesis examples.
- Classic symbiosis examples: clownfish/anemone, bees/flowers, lichen (fungus+alga) = mutualism; barnacles/whale, remora/shark, cattle egret/cattle, orchid/tree = commensalism; tick, flea, tapeworm, mistletoe = parasitism.

- Line graph for continuous change (over time); bar graph for comparing categories.
- Fire intensity = heat/energy released; fire **severity** = how much the fire changed/damaged the ecosystem (vegetation & soil loss; low/moderate/high). Big Basin: 77% high severity, 20% moderate, 2% low (study).
