## Topic 7 · What is an Ecosystem? {#t7}

<div class="callout know" markdown="1">
**Know it cold:** Ecosystem = **all the living things in an area + their nonliving environment, interacting**. A healthy ecosystem is in **equilibrium** (balance). Biotic = living (or once-living); abiotic = nonliving.
</div>

### Ecosystem {#s7-ecosystem}

An **ecosystem** is all the organisms (the **community**) in an area **interacting with each other and with the nonliving (abiotic) parts** of their environment — sunlight, water, air, rocks, and soil. "**Eco**" means **home**: an ecosystem is the system where organisms live. Ecosystems come in **different sizes** — a pond, a forest, a rotting log, or the whole Earth.

[[fig:levels-org|Levels of organization. Each level includes all the levels inside it.]]

| Level | What it includes | Big Basin example |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | one individual | one mule deer |
| Population | all members of **one species** in an area | the herd of mule deer |
| Community | **all the populations** (all species) in an area — living only | deer, pumas, redwoods, fungi, banana slugs… |
| **Ecosystem** | community **+ abiotic factors** | the community + creek water, sunlight, soil, temperature |
| Biome | a large region with a similar climate and communities | temperate coniferous forest |
| Biosphere | all ecosystems on Earth | the living part of the planet |

<div class="callout trap" markdown="1">
**Community vs. ecosystem:** a **community** is only the living things. An **ecosystem** adds the **nonliving** environment. If the answer choice mentions water, soil, or sunlight, it's describing an ecosystem.
</div>

### Equilibrium and resilience {#s7-equilibrium}

**Equilibrium** = a **balance** among the species in an ecosystem: populations rise and fall a little but stay within their **normal ranges** over time (a *dynamic* balance — like a population fluctuating around its carrying capacity).

| Disrupts equilibrium | Restores or protects equilibrium |
|---|---|
| removing a **keystone species** (wolves gone → deer 15,000 in Atlas NP) | reintroducing the keystone species (wolves back → all populations normal) |
| **habitat fragmentation** | wildlife corridors, protecting large habitat blocks |
| severe disturbances (high-severity fire, flood), drought, climate change | **succession**; fire adaptations like redwood bark (**resilience mechanisms**) |
| pollution (waste → algae bloom), invasive species | restoration by people (**tertiary** succession, prescribed fire) |

**Resilience** = an ecosystem's ability to **recover** after a disturbance. Higher **biodiversity** usually means higher resilience.

### Biotic vs. abiotic factors {#s7-biotic}

[[fig:biotic-abiotic|Living (or once-living) parts are biotic; nonliving physical and chemical parts are abiotic.]]

| **Biotic** (living or once living) | **Abiotic** (nonliving) |
|---|---|
| plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, algae | sunlight, temperature, water, air (O₂, CO₂) |
| **dead leaves, fallen logs** (once living) | rocks, soil minerals, pH, wind |
| biotic *interactions*: competition, predation, disease, members of the same species competing for food | weather and climate; abiotic "ecological forces or disturbances" such as **drought and fire** |

<div class="callout trap" markdown="1">
**Common traps**

- **Dead things** (a fallen log, dead leaves, a carcass) count as **biotic** in most class materials, because they were once living.
- **Soil is a mix:** the minerals and water are abiotic; the bacteria, worms, and decaying matter (humus) are biotic.
- **Weather is abiotic** (your Limiting Factors worksheet, Q10) — and it is also a density-independent limiting factor.
</div>

## Topic 8 · Graphs {#t8}

<div class="callout know" markdown="1">
**Know it cold — DRY MIX:** **D**ependent / **R**esponding variable goes on the **Y**-axis. **M**anipulated / **I**ndependent variable goes on the **X**-axis. **Time** is almost always the independent variable (x-axis).
</div>

### DRY MIX: axes, IV & DV {#s8-drymix}

[[fig:drymix|Where each variable belongs on a graph.]]

| Term | Meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent variable (IV)** = **manipulated** | What the scientist **changes or chooses** on purpose (often time) | **X-axis** (horizontal) |
| **Dependent variable (DV)** = **responding** | What is **measured**; it *responds to* (depends on) the IV | **Y-axis** (vertical) |
| **Constants** (controlled variables) | Everything kept **the same** so the test is fair | not graphed |
| **Control group** | The normal, untreated group used for comparison | compared on the graph |

| Investigation | IV (x-axis) | DV (y-axis) | Some constants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bean "rabbit" simulation | Time (years/rounds) | Rabbit population | grid size, drop height, the 1 / 2–4 / 5+ rules |
| Habitat fragmentation simulation | % meadow (forest burned/cleared) | # of suitable cells, or % habitat available | grid size (100 cells), the species' habitat rules |
| Redwood sorrel & light | Hours of light per day | Plant height (cm) | water, soil, pot size, temperature |
| California wildfire graphs | Year | Number of large fires (≥10,000 acres), or acres/hectares burned | — |

**Graph checklist:** a **title** ("DV vs. IV" or "The Effect of IV on DV"), both axes **labeled with units**, an **even scale**, and data points plotted accurately.

**Describing a relationship:** **positive** = as x increases, y increases (bean years 0–3: 25 → 64 rabbits). **Negative** = as x increases, y decreases (bean years 5–6: 73 → 55; more % meadow → fewer suitable murrelet cells).

### Choosing the right graph {#s8-graphtypes}

[[fig:line-vs-bar|Line graphs show change over time; bar graphs compare separate categories.]]

| Graph type | Use it for | Example from this unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Line graph** | **Change over time** or continuous data; shows trends | rabbit population per year; number of large wildfires per year |
| **Bar graph** | Comparing **separate categories** | area burned by vegetation type; individuals per species |
| Pie chart | Parts of a whole (adds to 100%) | relative abundance of species in one community |

<div class="callout trap" markdown="1">
**From your teacher's feedback:** your bean-simulation graph was marked **"Wrong type of graph"** because it was a **bar graph**. Population **over time** should be a **line graph**.
</div>

### Reading and interpreting graphs {#s8-reading}

Use the class graph-analysis template every time:

| Step | What to write |
|---|---|
| Title & type | What is the graph about? Line, bar, or pie? |
| X-axis | label, units, range (e.g., Year · years · 2000–2020) |
| Y-axis | label, units, range (e.g., Number of large fires · fires · 0–40) |
| Trend | The general direction and shape (increasing, decreasing, cyclical) |
| Points of interest | Specific points — use **numbers and units from both axes** |
| High / Low | The highest and lowest points |
| Pattern / Anomaly | What repeats — and what **doesn't fit** the pattern |
| Analysis | What you **can** conclude — and what you **cannot** |

[[fig:fire-trend|Practice data (illustrative). Try the template before reading the worked answers below.]]

<div class="callout model" markdown="1">
**Worked reading of the practice graph:**

- **Trend:** the number of large fires generally **increases** from 2000 to 2020 (the dashed trendline slopes up), with ups and downs.
- **High:** 2020, with **37** large fires. **Low:** 2010, with **6**.
- **Anomaly:** 2019 drops to **11** — between two high years (28 in 2018 and 37 in 2020).
- **Can conclude:** large fires became more common over this period.
- **Cannot conclude:** *why* — the graph alone doesn't prove what **caused** the increase (correlation is not causation), and it can't tell you the exact number for a future year.
</div>

## Topic 9 · Scientific Argumentation (CER) {#t9}

<div class="callout know" markdown="1">
**Know it cold:** **Claim** = what? (one sentence that answers the question). **Evidence** = how do you know? (specific data, **with units**). **Reasoning** = why does the evidence support the claim? (a **science concept**).
</div>

### Claim, evidence, reasoning {#s9-cer}

[[fig:cer-diagram|Evidence supports the claim; reasoning explains WHY, using a science idea.]]

| Part | What it is | ✔ Strong | ✘ Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | **One sentence** that directly answers the scientific question | "Wolves are the keystone species in Atlas National Park." | "Animals are important." (vague) · "I think it might be bees or wolves." (doesn't commit) · a sentence full of data (that's evidence) |
| **Evidence** | Specific data from the investigation — **quantitative (numbers with units)** and **qualitative** (observations); relevant and enough | "After wolves were reintroduced, deer fell from 15,000 to 7,000 and grass rose from 400 to 850 blades/m² in 5 years." | "The numbers changed a lot." · "Grass went from 400 to 850." (no units) · opinions or facts unrelated to the question |
| **Reasoning** | Explains **why** the evidence supports the claim using a **scientific concept** | "Wolves are a keystone predator: they limit deer, so grass recovers, which supports bees and other species — a trophic cascade." | Just repeats the evidence · "Because wolves are cool." · no science idea |

**Science concepts that make strong reasoning in this unit:** keystone species & trophic cascade · the 10% rule & metabolism (heat) · carrying capacity & limiting factors · density-dependent vs. density-independent · habitat fragmentation (edge vs. interior) · richness & evenness → resilience · primary vs. secondary succession.

<div class="callout model" markdown="1">
**Worked CER 1 — Bean simulation. Question: What is the carrying capacity of rabbits in the habitat?**

- **Claim:** The carrying capacity of the habitat is about 50 rabbits.
- **Evidence:** The population overshot to 64 rabbits in year 3 and 73 rabbits in year 5, then fell each time, and it stayed between 50 and 55 rabbits from year 6 to year 8.
- **Reasoning:** When the population is above carrying capacity, limiting factors (crowding — 5 or more rabbits in a square die, a density-dependent factor) push it back down. A population that rises and falls around one level is fluctuating around its carrying capacity.

**Worked CER 2 — Sampling Diversity. Question: Which community is more even, A or B?**

- **Claim:** Community B is more even than Community A.
- **Evidence:** In Community B, every species makes up between 0.08 and 0.35 of the 100 individuals. In Community A, one species makes up 0.625 of the 200 individuals while another is only 0.015.
- **Reasoning:** Evenness measures how similar the population sizes of the species are. B's species are all close to an equal share (0.20 each for 5 species), while A is dominated by a single species.

**Worked CER 3 — Habitat fragmentation. Question: How does losing forest affect habitat for the marbled murrelet?**

- **Claim:** As more forest is burned or cleared, the habitat available to marbled murrelets drops — faster than the forest itself.
- **Evidence:** When forest fell from 90 to 20 cells (−78%), suitable cells fell from 90 to 4 (−96%), and the habitat available fell from 100% to 20%.
- **Reasoning:** Habitat fragmentation leaves small, isolated patches that are mostly edge. Species that need larger patches of intact forest can't use them, so usable habitat shrinks faster than total forest area.
</div>

<div class="callout tip" markdown="1">
**Multiple-choice strategy for CER questions:**

- **"Best claim?"** → the one-sentence choice that answers *exactly* the question asked, with no data.
- **"Best evidence?"** → specific, relevant **numbers with units** from the data given.
- **"Best reasoning?"** → the choice that names a **science concept** and connects it to the evidence — not one that just restates the numbers.
</div>
