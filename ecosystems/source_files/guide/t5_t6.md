## Topic 5 · Succession, Habitat Fragmentation & Fire {#t5}

<div class="callout know" markdown="1">
**Know it cold:** **No soil → primary** succession (starts with lichens). **Soil left → secondary** succession (starts with grasses and wildflowers; faster). **Soil lost + humans restore → tertiary** (Ms. Hero's term). **Surface fires** burn the forest floor; **crown fires** burn the treetops. **Fragmentation** breaks habitat into small, isolated patches.
</div>

### Habitat fragmentation {#s5-fragmentation}

**Habitat fragmentation** = breaking a large, continuous habitat into **smaller, isolated patches** (and often shrinking it), caused by human activities (roads, development, farming, logging) or natural causes like **fire**.

[[fig:frag-concept|Fragmentation turns one big habitat into small patches with more edge and less interior. Corridors reconnect them.]]

**Why it matters:**

- Smaller, isolated populations → animals can't move to find food, mates, or new territory → less genetic variety.
- **Less interior habitat, more edge** (edges are brighter, hotter, windier, and easier for predators and invasive species to reach).
- Species that need **large areas** (pumas, interior-forest birds) decline first.
- It disrupts the ecosystem's **equilibrium** and lowers **carrying capacity**.

### Primary succession {#s5-primary}

**Ecological succession** = the gradual, fairly predictable change in the species living in an area over time.

**Primary succession** = building an ecosystem **where there is NO soil** — bare rock such as **glacier-scraped granite (Yosemite)**, new **lava flows**, new volcanic islands, or rock exposed by a landslide.

**Pioneer species** = the very first organisms to colonize an area. In primary succession they are **lichens** (a fungus + alga mutualism) and mosses: tough, and they don't need soil. Lichens **chemically and physically break rock into tiny pieces**; their remains add organic matter → **the first soil**.

[[fig:primary-succession|From your "Life from Stone" timeline: soil deepens and biodiversity increases, while light reaching the ground decreases.]]

<div class="callout handout" markdown="1">
**From "Ecological Succession: Life from Stone — How Yosemite was Made":** Yosemite's granite formed deep underground from magma, was pushed up to the surface, and **glaciers carved huge valleys** and scraped the rock bare before melting away. That bare granite is where primary succession began.

| Stage | Pioneer | Early intermediate | Late intermediate | Climax community |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approx. years | 0–150 | 150–500 | 500–1,000 | 1,000+ |
| Organisms | lichen, crevice-dwelling plants | grasses & wildflowers; primary consumers | shrubs & small trees; secondary consumers | large trees; tertiary consumers & apex predators |
| Soil | tiny rock bits — not soil yet | gravelly, shallow soil | moderate depth & quality | deep, rich soil |
| Light at ground | maximum | slightly less | moderate (shade from small trees) | little (shade crowds out sun-loving species) |
| Ecological impact | break bare rock into tiny pieces → foundation of soil | add organic matter & nitrogen; hold soil | add nitrogen, trap moisture; decomposers speed up breakdown | very diverse, stable community |
</div>

### Secondary & tertiary succession {#s5-secondary}

**Secondary succession** = an established ecosystem **recovering after a disturbance that removed the community but left the SOIL** — after a fire, flood, logging, a hurricane, or an abandoned farm field. It is **faster** than primary succession because soil, buried seeds (the **seed bank**), and roots are already there.

**Tertiary succession (Ms. Hero's term):** the soil is **NOT intact** (severely eroded or degraded) and **humans intervene to restore** the ecosystem — replanting, adding soil or mulch, erosion control. This term is rarely in textbooks, so **use Ms. Hero's definition on her test**.

### Who lives there: pioneers, climax community & more {#s5-organisms}

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Pioneer species** | First organisms to colonize; tough; build or hold soil | primary: **lichens, mosses**; secondary: **grasses, wildflowers** |
| **Intermediate species** | Move in as soil improves | shrubs, small trees, primary & secondary consumers |
| **Climax community** | The final, mature, relatively **stable** community; it lasts until the next big disturbance | old-growth redwood forest in Big Basin: large trees, tertiary consumers, apex predators |

From Life from Stone Q6, organisms in the climax community fall into three groups:

| Symbol | Group | Who |
|---|---|---|
| ★ | **Residents** | persist in the climax community — large trees, tertiary consumers, apex predators |
| ✓ | **Edge-dwellers** | live along edges and openings — grasses, wildflowers, shrubs, small trees, and the consumers that use them |
| ✗ | **Retired pioneers** | early colonizers that mostly disappear as shade increases — lichens, crevice-dwelling plants |

### Fire: surface vs. crown, severity & disturbances {#s5-fire}

A **disturbance** is an event that disrupts an ecosystem's community — **natural** (fire, flood, hurricane, volcano, landslide, drought) or **human** (logging, clearing land, development, farming, pollution). Disturbances start succession.

[[fig:fire-types|Ground, surface, and crown fires. Ladder fuels carry a surface fire up into the crowns.]]

| **Crown fire** | **Both** | **Surface fire** |
|---|---|---|
| Burns through the **tree tops (canopy)**, spreading tree to tree | Are wildfires and **disturbances** | Burns along the **forest floor** — grass, needles, fallen branches, shrubs |
| Usually very hot and fast; pushed by wind and slope | Need **fuel + heat + oxygen** | Lower flames; the **canopy is mostly untouched** |
| **Kills most trees** — usually **high severity** | Driven by weather (heat, wind, dryness) and slope | Big, thick-barked trees usually **survive** — usually **low to moderate severity** |
| Often climbs up **ladder fuels** (shrubs, low branches) | Release smoke and CO₂ | **Clears fuel**; soil stays healthy; stored seeds can sprout |
| Can damage soil → slower recovery | Are followed by **succession** | Can be good for the forest (natural or **prescribed**) |

A **ground fire** smolders below the surface in the duff (partly decayed litter) and roots.

**Fire severity vs. fire intensity:**

| Term | Measures | Think of it as |
|---|---|---|
| **Fire intensity** | How much **heat/energy** the fire releases | The fire's *behavior* |
| **Fire severity** | How much the fire **changed or damaged the ecosystem** — plants killed and soil burned (low / moderate / high) | The fire's *effects* |

[[fig:fire-severity|Severity describes what the fire did to the ecosystem. Big Basin 2020 was mostly high severity.]]

**Prescribed (controlled) burns** are low-severity surface fires set on purpose to burn up built-up fuel, so that a future wildfire is less likely to become a devastating crown fire.

## Topic 6 · Biodiversity {#t6}

<div class="callout know" markdown="1">
**Know it cold:** **Richness** = how many species. **Evenness** = how equal their numbers are. **Relative abundance** = one species' count ÷ total count. The most biodiverse communities have high richness **and** high evenness.
</div>

### What biodiversity is and why it matters {#s6-biodiversity}

**Biodiversity** (biological diversity) = the **variety of life** — the variety of ecosystems, species, and genes in an area (or on Earth).

**Why it matters:** ecosystems with more biodiversity are usually more **stable and resilient**. If one species declines, others can fill its role (for example, a predator has other prey to switch to), so the food web doesn't collapse, and the ecosystem recovers faster after a disturbance.

**How scientists measure it:** they can't count every organism, so they **sample** — count individuals in sample plots (quadrats), along lines (transects), or from aerial counts (like the HHMI elephant census video) — and use the samples to estimate richness, evenness, and relative abundance.

### Richness and evenness {#s6-richeven}

| Measure | Question it answers | High | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Species richness** | How many **different species** are there? | rainforest — very rich | a field of only one crop |
| **Species evenness** | Are the species' population sizes **similar**, or does one dominate? | every species has about the same count | a **grass lawn**: grass far outnumbers clover, dandelions, ants, and bees |

[[fig:fields-mice|Practice like Figure 1 on your Sampling Diversity handout: label each field rich, even, both, or neither.]]

| Field | Species counts | Rich? | Even? | Label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W | 16 (one species) | No | — (only one species) | **neither** |
| X | 8, 8 | No (2 species) | **Yes** | even |
| Y | 13, 1, 1, 1 | **Yes** (4 species) | No | rich |

<div class="callout trap" markdown="1">
**Common traps:** the **total number of individuals** (16 in every field) tells you nothing about richness. Richness counts **species**, not individuals. And a community can be rich but uneven (Y) or even but not rich (X).
</div>

### Relative abundance (with your class data) {#s6-relabund}

**Relative abundance = (number of individuals of ONE species) ÷ (total individuals of ALL species)**

Multiply by 100 for a percent. All relative abundances in a community add up to 1 (100%).

**Example (Community A, species B):** 125 ÷ 200 = **0.625 = 62.5%**.

**Quick evenness check:** if every species were equal, each would have a relative abundance of 1 ÷ (number of species). For 5 species, that's 0.20 each. The closer all the values are to that number, the more even the community. If one species is more than half of everything, the community is very uneven.

**Q3 — richness or evenness, which matters more?** A strong answer: **both** matter for a healthy ecosystem. Richness gives the food web many backup species; evenness means no single species dominates. A lawn is a weak, unstable community: if a disease hit the grass, almost everything would be gone. Use CER: *claim* → *evidence from the tables* → *reasoning about resilience*.
